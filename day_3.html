Capstone Day 3 - Survival Guide

Diving into your year of student internship/teaching will expose you to a host of new experiences, regardless of your background. Here are some thoughts that may help you in your journey.

Mistakes are good

As teachers we should strive to model by example. When my students make mistakes, that’s good. It’s part of the learning process. It is a mistake that other students likely have made/would make as well. But when we make mistakes as teachers, we tend to be less forgiving of ourselves. Lead by example. Use your mistakes as lessons to learn from. Treat them as opportunities. They are not a reflection of your shortcomings or lack of ability. They are part of the process.

It’s so easy to say. But if you can actually do it, and use it to demonstrate by example, it also makes students more comfortable with being wrong. And this is one of the key prerequisites for a learning environment conducive to meaningful and self-initiated learning.

Relationships are good

Work hard to develop your relationships with everyone in the school from day one. Teachers (not only your mentor teacher) – get to know them. Learn their names. Be annoying so they remember yours. Principals. Other administrators in the front office. Students. Friends of students in your class. Develop relationships with all of them. You will be dropped in as a relatively new face, given the timing of placements and the school year, and will be a novelty. Leverage that. Get to know everyone.

Relationships can help you professionally in the long run. Reference letters, job referrals, internal applications, all are easier with a multitude of good relationships. Talk about things other than school. Students learn better from people that they like.

Planning is good

Nothing beats a good lesson plan. If you have a vision for the lesson from start to finish, with clearly defined learning objectives and metrics by which to measure their achievement, then 90% of the work is already done. A good lesson plan is like the plot to a good film – it has an arc to it that follows a natural progression. Each step of the process should be included intentionally, and they all should build towards achieving your stated learning objective. While sooner or later you may abandon the traditional paper lesson plan that maps out the lesson down to the minute, the best lessons will still be developed with the key elements those in mind.

Beyond the content itself, a well-constructed plan serves as your primary tool for classroom management. When you aren’t preoccupied with "what comes next" or searching for a specific resource, you have the mental bandwidth to actually engage with your students. It gives you the freedom to move around the room, gauge the energy level, and provide individual support where it’s needed most. Ultimately, the more work you put into the plan beforehand, the more present and responsive you can be during the delivery.

Flexibility is good

Even the most meticulously crafted lesson plan must survive the reality of the classroom. Flexibility isn't about a lack of preparation; it is the ability to adapt your preparation to the immediate needs of your students. When a technology failure occurs or a concept takes longer to grasp than anticipated, your ability to pivot gracefully ensures that learning never stops.

Embracing flexibility also means recognizing that the "hidden curriculum"—the social and emotional needs of your students—often takes precedence over the day’s slides. If a class discussion pivots toward a real-world event or a collective misunderstanding that requires immediate attention, don’t be afraid to deviate. These unplanned detours are often where the most impactful learning happens, as they demonstrate that you value the students' curiosity and lived experiences more than a rigid timeline.

Ultimately, your internship is about more than just surviving the curriculum. It’s about discovering who you are as an educator. By embracing your mistakes, prioritizing human connection, and balancing intentional planning with the courage to be flexible, you are doing the hard work of building a professional foundation. This year will be demanding, but it is also the first step into a career that is as rewarding as it is complex. Trust the process, trust your preparation, and remember that you belong in the room.
